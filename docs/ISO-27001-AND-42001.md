# Using pr-reviewer and pr-fixer under ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 42001

This document explains how to use the two skills in a way that supports an information security
management system (ISO/IEC 27001:2022) and an AI management system (ISO/IEC 42001:2023). It is
written for engineering leads, security officers and whoever owns the management system.

It is not legal advice and it does not claim compliance. Using these skills does not make an
organisation compliant with, or certified to, either standard. Certification is a decision by an
accredited body about an organisation's management system. Neither standard certifies a tool. Treat
the mapping as a starting point for your own risk assessment and Statement of Applicability, and have
a lead auditor or implementer with licensed copies of the standards confirm it.

## 1. What the standards ask of this use case

Reviewing and repairing pull requests with AI touches both standards.

- ISO/IEC 27001 cares about who can change source code, how changes are approved and tracked, how
  secrets and suppliers are handled, what is logged and how incidents are managed. The standard has no
  AI-specific control, so the general ones apply.
- ISO/IEC 42001 adds the AI layer: a policy for AI use, an impact assessment, an AI risk assessment,
  life-cycle controls such as verification, monitoring and event logs, controls for the responsible use
  of AI systems, and controls for third parties.

An AI agent that can read code, post a verdict and push commits is both a tool used inside your
development process and an AI system you operate. Both standards apply to it, and you can usually
produce one set of evidence for both.

## 2. How to run the skills so they support both standards

Do these before you turn on the automatic workflows.

1. Decide the rule for AI in code review and write it down. State that the AI verdict is advisory
   and that a named person approves and merges. State whether the AI counts as a reviewer for any
   minimum-reviewers rule. Put this in your AI policy (42001 clause 5.2, A.2.2) and your secure
   development procedure (27001 A.5.37, A.8.25).
2. Enforce it in the platform. Use branch protection or rulesets: require a human approval,
   require the `review-gate` check, block force-pushes, and restrict who can bypass. A procedure that
   the platform does not enforce is only an intention.
3. Give each bot its own identity and the least rights that work. The reviewer is read-only on
   code. The fixer can push to the pull request branch and nothing else. Neither can approve or merge.
   Keep tokens short-lived, owned by a named person, and listed in an inventory (27001 A.5.15, A.5.18,
   A.8.2, A.8.4).
4. Treat the model provider as a supplier. Do due diligence and sign an agreement. Check what the
   provider retains, whether it trains on your data, where it processes it, and how it tells you about
   incidents and model changes (27001 A.5.19 to A.5.23, 42001 A.10.3).
5. Decide what may be sent to the provider. Exclude repositories or paths that hold regulated or
   secret material. Classify code and comments (27001 A.5.12, A.5.14, A.8.12).
6. Run an AI risk assessment and an AI system impact assessment for this use, and record the
   conclusion (42001 clauses 6.1.2, 6.1.4, 8.2, 8.4, A.5). Put prompt injection, tool abuse, automation
   bias and over-reliance in the risk register with owners.
7. Set up logging and retention. Pull requests, labels and comments are working data that people
   with the right permission can edit. Export your platform audit log to protected storage, set a
   retention period and restrict who can edit or delete bot comments (27001 A.8.15, A.5.33, 42001
   A.6.2.8).
8. Put the skills and their configuration under change control. A new version of a skill, a prompt
   change, a model or effort change and a token-scope change are changes to an AI system. Record them
   and re-validate (27001 A.8.32, 42001 clause 6.3, A.6.2.5).
9. Define monitoring and escalation. Pick a few measures: defects the AI missed, false alarms,
   reverted fixes, escalation rate, cost. Name who receives escalations and how fast they respond
   (27001 A.8.16, 42001 A.6.2.6, clause 9.1).
10. Train the people in the loop. Approvers should know what the verdict covers and does not cover,
    and how automation bias works (27001 A.6.3, 42001 A.4.6).

## 3. Where the skills help

| Need | What the skills provide |
|---|---|
| Separation of duties between finding and fixing | Two skills with different rights. The fixer cannot judge its own work. |
| Bounded autonomy | A cycle budget of five, a hard ceiling of ten, and a question to a person with a five-minute window. Silence leaves the pull request blocked. |
| Traceability | Every verdict names the commit it read and the counts behind it. Every fix has a closure comment with the reproduction, the failing test and the passing run. |
| Human decision points | The merge, the extension answer, every refused finding judged by the reviewer, and every escalation. |
| Protection against forged results | An author allowlist for markers, and a required gate that runs from the base branch. |
| Resistance to prompt injection | Pull request text, comments and CI logs are treated as data. The work list comes only from the trusted verdict comment. |
| Evidence of model and effort | The coverage note and status comment record the tier, model and effort in force, and any substitution. |

## 4. What the skills do not do

- They do not run your risk assessment, impact assessment, Statement of Applicability, internal audit
  or management review.
- They do not make labels and comments tamper-evident. Export audit logs yourself.
- They do not assess your model provider or check that provider certificates cover the service you use.
- They do not make legal determinations on personal data, intellectual property or sector rules.
- They have not been validated by an auditor.

## 5. Control mapping and sources

The rest of this document is the control-level mapping. The research behind it was done on
2026-10-14. ISO texts are paywalled, so control numbers and titles were checked in two secondary
sources each, and status on ISO.org pages. Anything that could not be verified is marked `unverified`.


Research date: 2026-10-14. Scope: an AI reviewer (read-only, posts a verdict marker), an AI fixer (edits code on a branch and pushes), human escalation, labels and comments as audit trail, a CI gate as required check, secrets, third-party model providers, logging, human oversight, change management.

How to read this file:

- "Supports" means the practice can produce evidence or reduce risk relevant to a control. It never means the control is satisfied. Only a risk assessment, a Statement of Applicability, and an audit can decide that.
- The practice rows describe what the skills do or what an organisation must configure around them. The control research was done from a short description of the skills, so check each row against the files in this repository before you rely on it.
- Control numbers and titles were verified as described in section 1. Where something could not be verified it is marked `unverified`.
- ISO text is paywalled. The normative text itself was not read. Annex B guidance text of ISO/IEC 42001 and the guidance text of ISO/IEC 27002 were not read, so statements about what a control "expects" are paraphrased from secondary sources or marked `unverified`.

### Where each standard does the work

- ISO/IEC 27001 gives the access, supplier, logging, incident and change-management controls. It was written before agentic AI and has no AI-specific control. The mapping above relies on general controls.
- ISO/IEC 42001 gives the AI-specific wrapper: impact assessment (6.1.4, 8.4, A.5), AI risk assessment (6.1.2, 8.2), life-cycle controls (A.6), responsible use (A.9) and third parties (A.10).
- Overlap is large for logging (8.15 and A.6.2.8), suppliers (5.19 to 5.23 and A.10), incidents (5.24 to 5.28 and A.8.4) and change (8.32 and A.6.2.5). One shared process can produce evidence for both, but the auditor of each standard decides whether it is enough.

## 3. Limits

What these skills cannot do for an ISO audit:

1. Using the skills does not make an organisation compliant with, or certified to, ISO/IEC 27001 or ISO/IEC 42001. Certification is a decision by an accredited certification body about an organisation's management system. Neither standard certifies a tool.
2. Annex A controls are a reference set, not a checklist. The organisation must run its own risk assessment (27001 clause 6.1.2, 42001 clause 6.1.2), compare its chosen controls with Annex A, justify exclusions, and keep a Statement of Applicability. No skill does this. (The Statement of Applicability in 6.1.3 is verified for 27001 at Modulos and in secondary summaries for both standards. The 42001 clause text was not read.)
3. The management-system clauses sit above any tool: scope (4.3), policy (5.2), objectives (6.2), competence (7.2), internal audit (9.2), management review (9.3) and corrective action (10.2). The skills supply operating evidence at most.
4. Audit evidence quality is the organisation's problem. Labels and comments are working data. Whether they count as records depends on retention, integrity and access control that live outside the skills.
5. Model providers sit outside the control of the skills. Supplier assessment (27001 5.19 to 5.23, 42001 A.10.3) needs contracts, due diligence and monitoring that the organisation performs. Provider certificates cover a stated scope only; check that the scope includes the service in use.
6. The skills cannot make a legal determination on personal data, intellectual property or sector rules. Those fall under 27001 5.31 and 5.34 and under law. A DPIA or any AI regulation obligation is a separate legal exercise. GDPR and the EU AI Act were not researched here, so no statement about them is made.
7. The mapping is a reading of control titles and secondary summaries. The normative text and the Annex B guidance were not read. A lead auditor or implementer with licensed copies should confirm it before it is used in an SoA or an audit.
